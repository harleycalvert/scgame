How to play

  1. Place. Take turns putting your three smileys on empty spots.
  2. Move. When all three of yours are down, each turn slide one smiley along a line to the empty spot next to it. You can't move until all yours are placed.
  3. Win. Get three of your smileys in a row along a line. On the circle, three neighbouring spots round the rim also count.

If it's your turn and none of your smileys can move, you lose. If the same position comes up three times, it's a draw.

The games behind it

Moving three-in-a-row games turn up all over the world. These are the main relatives of the one you're playing.

Three Men's Morris Europe & beyond

This is the smallest of the "mill" games, the family that includes Six and Nine Men's Morris. "Morris" probably comes from the Latin merellus, meaning a counter or game piece. The name "Three Men's Morris" seems to be fairly modern, borrowed from its bigger cousin.

The Romans played a three-stone game, sometimes called terni lapilli. Some people think the small game the poet Ovid mentions in his Art of Love is this one. You'll often read that the board is carved into an Egyptian temple roof at Kurna from around 1400 BC. Historians are doubtful, though, because the same stone also has Coptic Christian crosses carved on it, so the scratches can't be dated.

The evidence from medieval England is much clearer. Morris boards were scratched into the stone benches of cathedral cloisters, probably by monks or choirboys passing the time.

Tapatan Philippines

Tapatan is the same game on the square board with diagonals, which is your square board with 3 each. The American ethnographer Stewart Culin described it in 1900, in a study of Philippine games. Players used three pebbles, or bits of bark or wood, on a square divided into eight sections.

The board might be a wooden one, or just lines marked on a floor or a doorstep. Each player has three pieces, traditionally light wood against dark wood. Culin also linked it to a Chinese game from Canton called luk tsut k'i, or "six man chess", which is the same game again.

Achi Ghana

Achi uses the same board but gives each player four pieces. Once all eight are down there's only one gap left, so every move is a race to steer that single hole where you want it. Try it with "4 each" on the square.

The board historian H. J. R. Murray recorded Achi from Ghana in the 1920s. A version was also played by the Yoruba in Nigeria. Children often scratch the board into the dirt and play with pebbles, seeds or little sticks. One player keeps the bark on their sticks and the other peels theirs, which is a neat way to tell the two sides apart.

Rules vary from place to place. Some sources describe Achi with only three pieces each, which makes it the same as Three Men's Morris.

The circle board A variant

Game collections list a "circular" Three Men's Morris among the relatives. On this circle the spots connect exactly as on the square: round the rim, and in through the centre. The difference is that the rim curves, so any three neighbouring rim spots also make a row. That gives 12 ways to win instead of 8.
